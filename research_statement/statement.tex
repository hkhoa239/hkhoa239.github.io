\section*{Research Area and Approach}

I completed the Honors Bachelor's program in Computer Science at Ho Chi Minh City University of Technology, Vietnam National University, with the official diploma expected in November 2026. I am seeking PhD training for Spring or Fall 2027 in \textbf{computer vision, vision--language models, and vision--language--action (VLA) models}.

My general question is how a robot can perceive, remember, and act when the scene is only partly observed and keeps changing. Recent VLAs show that one policy can map images and language to robot actions and generalize beyond a single lab setup~\cite{black2025pi05,bu2025univla}. They are still expensive to run, weak when the needed evidence has left the camera frame, and hard to evaluate outside a hand-built simulator. My future work sits at those three gaps: \textbf{optimizing VLAs}, \textbf{memory-augmented VLAs}, and \textbf{video world models as simulation}.

\section*{Current Research}

A recurring theme in my experiments is that a foundation model is useful only when we know which evidence it should trust.

In reliable few-point metric grounding, under review, I study whether a monocular depth model can be anchored by a handful of external measurements, and whether that anchor survives when some measurements are wrong. On a standard indoor benchmark, a small set of observations produces a large drop in absolute relative error, and most of that gain remains when the majority of the observations are corrupted. Acquisition-aware anomaly detection asks a related question: blur, motion, illumination, and sensor noise can look like defects even when the object has not changed. We introduce a benchmark for those acquisition shifts and a training-free detector that separates a change in capture from evidence of a real defect. Mutual hand--object reasoning, under review, moves closer to manipulation. Instead of reasoning only from the hand to the object, the model lets the two refine each other, and it improves egocentric segmentation when the test scene differs from training. I also contributed the vision--language prompt-optimization experiments in PEAR, a physics-reasoning framework that grounds a problem in an executable ontology before it computes an answer.

These projects are perception experiments in computer vision and vision--language models. They are also the reason I am moving toward robot learning. With Do Van Minh's group at SMART (Singapore--MIT Alliance for Research and Technology) and ARENA Lab at Singapore Management University, I have started two lines that connect that perception to action. Video-to-simulation asks whether computer vision can recover object geometry and hand--object interaction from ordinary human video, then place that interaction in a robot simulator. MemoryPi asks whether a VLA can store and retrieve what is no longer visible, instead of acting only on the current frame. My undergraduate thesis, graded 9.84/10, studied scheduling for concurrent vision inference. It taught me to treat speed and evaluation as part of the research question.

\section*{Future Research Directions}

\subsection*{Optimizing Vision--Language--Action Models}

Models such as $\pi_{0.5}$ show that a VLA can be aimed at open-world generalization rather than one tabletop setup~\cite{black2025pi05}. UniVLA pushes further by learning task-centric latent actions from human and robot video, so a policy is not tied to a single embodiment~\cite{bu2025univla}. The cost of that generality is inference. A VLA that recomputes a full visual representation at every control step is difficult to run in real time. VLA-Cache shows that much of the image barely changes between frames: it reuses those static tokens without retraining, recomputes the tokens the task still depends on, and reports up to a $1.7\times$ reduction in CUDA latency and a higher control frequency with negligible loss in success~\cite{xu2025vlacache}.

I want to treat optimization as a question about \textit{what must be recomputed}, not only as an engineering speedup. Which visual tokens, language tokens, or action chunks actually change the next command? When is a frozen VLA better steered by a small outer controller than fine-tuned end to end? Harness VLA already takes one step in that direction: it leaves the VLA frozen and calls it as one primitive among analytic moves~\cite{zhang2026harness}. My thesis on concurrent vision inference is the systems background for this question. The goal is a VLA that stays accurate while spending computation on the part of the scene the task still depends on.

\subsection*{Memory-Augmented Vision--Language--Action Models}

Current VLAs act well when the relevant object is still in view. Long-horizon tasks often are not like that. RoboMME makes the failure concrete. A generalist policy may need temporal memory (how many times something has happened), spatial memory (where an occluded object went), object memory (which item a brief cue referred to), or procedural memory (how to repeat a demonstration)~\cite{dai2026robomme}. A longer context window is not the same as any of these. Harness VLA takes a complementary route. Its agent stores successful command traces for a task and reusable rules about what succeeded or failed, then chooses the next primitive~\cite{zhang2026harness}.

MemoryPi is my early version of this question. The policy should write down a subtask outcome, a spatial relation, or a failure, and read it back when the camera no longer shows the evidence. The research problems are what to store, when to write and retrieve it, and how to condition action generation on that memory without making inference too expensive. A useful result is a gain on tasks where the decisive observation has already disappeared, together with an account of which kind of memory produced the gain.

\subsection*{Video World Models as Simulation}

Physics simulators are strong where the assets exist, and silent where they do not. A complementary route is to learn the scene from video. Feed-forward geometry models such as VGGT recover cameras, depth, points, and tracks from one or many views~\cite{wang2025vggt}. VGGT-$\Omega$ shows that this reconstruction scales to static and dynamic scenes, and that the learned scene representation can improve a VLA~\cite{wang2026vggtomega}. Single-image reconstruction such as SAM 3D recovers object shape, texture, and layout in clutter~\cite{sam3d2025}. On top of that geometry, human video can become robot experience. RoboEdit turns a human manipulation video into a physically plausible robot video with aligned 3D hand state~\cite{guo2026roboedit}. Do as I Do retargets everyday monocular video onto a dexterous hand~\cite{paliwal2026doasido}. Real2Render2Real builds demonstrations from a phone scan and one human video, without running object dynamics or teleoperating a robot, in a form a VLA can train on~\cite{yu2025r2r2r}. Contact-wrench guidance such as CHORD then uses human demonstration to train dexterous policies in simulation~\cite{zhu2026chord}.

I am equally interested in the learned dynamics, not only the reconstructed assets. Cosmos 3 treats language, image, video, audio, and action as one omnimodal world model, so the same backbone can understand, generate video, and propose actions~\cite{nvidia2026cosmos3}. V-JEPA 2 shows that a video model pretrained by observation, then post-trained as a latent action-conditioned world model on less than 62 hours of unlabeled robot video, can plan to image goals on a robot it was not trained on~\cite{assran2025vjepa2}. The open problem is reliability. A world model used as a simulator must be right on actions the policy should \textit{not} take, which are rare in successful demonstrations. World Action Verifier makes that gap explicit: it checks whether a predicted future is plausible and whether the action could have reached it, and uses that check to improve the model~\cite{liu2026wav}.

This is the evaluation question I want to study. Video-to-simulation and action-conditioned video should complement asset-based simulators, not replace them. The claim is not that a generated future looks real. The claim is when that future is stable enough to train or score a VLA, and when it is not.

\section*{Summary}

My near-term plan is to optimize how a VLA spends computation, to give it a memory of what the current frame no longer shows, and to test video world models as simulators for scenes we cannot author by hand. I am especially interested in unstructured settings, including construction and industrial work, where the visual conditions vary and a full simulator is hard to build in advance. The perception experiments above are the starting point: a policy should know which visual evidence to trust before it decides what to remember and how to act.
